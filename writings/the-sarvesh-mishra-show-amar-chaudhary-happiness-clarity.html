Today we have doctors, healers and more information than any generation before us, yet we still feel stressed and unwell. Why? That is the question host Sarvesh Mishra opens this episode of The Sarvesh Mishra Show with. His guest is thinker, corporate trainer and keynote speaker Amar Chaudhary, who trains police personnel (from sub-inspectors to senior officers), teachers and corporate teams. He uses simple examples, stories and jokes to show that most of life’s complications are of our own making.

Key takeaways
  • Happiness is a decision, success is a process. Don’t wire them to the same switch.
  • Circumstances are like a rope: the same rope can pull you out of a pit or become a noose.
  • Being grateful for what you have makes you lighter.
  • A good leader takes responsibility, shares credit and listens.
  • You can’t control your thoughts, but you can control your words. Change your words and your thinking follows — in his view.
  • The IAS formula for choosing a career: Interest, Ability, Scope. Meet a professional counsellor before acting on it.

1. To play the hero, we created our own villains

Amar Chaudhary’s first answer to Sarvesh Mishra’s opening question is that the world has far fewer problems than we have manufactured. His argument is a fun one: everyone wants to be a hero, and a hero needs a villain. Bollywood heroes fight terrorism, poverty and corruption, while Hollywood invented imaginary threats like aliens. In our own lives we do the same: we create problems first, then fight them so we can feel brave.

He adds that we look at problems through a magnifying glass. Life is like curd: add some sugar or spice and enjoy it. If you inspect it under a microscope before eating, all you will see are germs, and you won’t be able to eat it. His conclusion is that our own thinking becomes our worst enemy.

He also has a different view on struggle stories. His advice is to subtract 50 to 60 percent from any successful person’s struggle story, because stories are usually told more dramatically than they actually happened.

2. Life is simple; it takes effort to bend it

Keeping a flower straight takes no effort, but bending it takes force. Amar Chaudhary says life works the same way. We put in needless effort and make it complicated. In his view, the first step is accepting that life is simple at its core.

3. Keep the happiness switch separate from the success switch

In the old days, a fan and a bulb were often wired to a single switch, so if one failed, both went out. Amar Chaudhary says we have done the same with success and happiness. We have put a “conditions apply” star on our happiness: I’ll be happy if this happens, I’ll be happy if that happens.

Success is like growing a beard: it takes time. Happiness is like shaving: you can decide right now and start being happy.

So his advice is not to wait for a particular situation before being happy. He is also clear that problems can stop you from succeeding, but they cannot stop you from smiling.

4. Circumstances are like a rope

Ask “what do you need to dry clothes?” and most people say “a rope”, when the real answer is sunshine. Amar Chaudhary uses this to show that our minds hold many “ropes”, beliefs that aren’t actually necessary.

He also plays on the Hindi word for circumstance, “paristhiti”, where “par” means “dependent on someone else”. The same rope can pull you out of a pit or be used as a noose. The rope doesn’t decide; you do.

He illustrates this with a joke: robbers draw a circle around a man, tell him to stay inside or be killed, and rob his wife. The man is laughing, because while they were robbing her, he had stepped out of the circle twice. The message is that a change in outlook changes the meaning of the same situation.

5. Gratitude: respecting what you already have

Amar Chaudhary breaks down the Hindi word for gratitude, “aabhaar”, into aa + bhaar, where “bhaar” means weight. The moment you thank someone, you become lighter, and being light is the first condition for rising higher. He notes that when you thank someone for a glass of water, the usual reply is “would you like some more?” and says the same happens with God.

He argues that failing to value what we have is a big mistake. At a wedding, the host asks where the kids are if they didn’t come, or where your wife is if she didn’t come, but if all three of you show up, nobody asks anything. The ones who arrived get less respect than the ones who didn’t.

Speaking about telekinesis, he says objects have a kind of “memory”, which is why we bow to a stage, to weapons or to a new car. In his view, those who take care of what they have attract what they want. This is his own perspective, which he explains in his own style.

6. Leader, boss and follower: what’s the difference?

Sarvesh Mishra describes three types of people: leaders, boss and followers. Amar Chaudhary says fewer people become leaders because when applause comes, so do the brickbats, and both land on the leader. But he adds that followers face just as many challenges. The people walking behind Gandhi on the Dandi March felt the same pebbles and the same heat; they only avoided the responsibility. Everybody knows who first stepped on the moon, far fewer know who followed six seconds later.

He names two key qualities of a leader:

  • Take responsibility, not credit. Using the example of prasad, he says the one who keeps just a laddoo or two out of twenty and distributes the rest is the leader, while the one who keeps most for himself is, in his words, a beggar.
  • Listen. Take the English word HEART and remove the T, and you get HEAR. The word SENIOR also hides an EAR. In his words, a senior is someone with big ears.

7. The solution to 90% of problems: listen

Amar Chaudhary claims every relationship has the same complaint: “they don’t listen.” This applies to teacher and student, parent and child, senior and junior, and husband and wife. His advice is to listen to a few people (parents, boss, seniors, juniors, children), or else you will end up listening to the whole world. In the early days of a romance, couples listen to each other for hours, and after marriage they stop. That, he says, is where many relationships begin to fall apart.

Treat the “90%” figure as the guest’s illustration, not a measured statistic. Related: T.S. Madan on pain and relationships.

8. Clarity matters even more than talent

Amar Chaudhary says clarity is a bigger power than talent. His example: even the world’s best driver in a car with a foggy windshield will lose to an average driver with a clear one. He cites Arjuna, Narendra becoming Vivekananda, Angulimala and Gandhi, saying none of them gained new talent; they simply gained clarity.

His top tool for clarity is meditation. People call it boring, but he asks why you’d want to make it fun if you want to be a millionaire and a great person. In his view, challenging work is where success lies.

9. “Badlaav” (change) as “bad + labh”

Looking for success in easy work is like searching for a ring under the street lamp instead of where you dropped it. He splits the Hindi word for change, “badlaav”, into “bad-labh”: to reach the gain (labh), you must pass through the “bad”, the tough and challenging part, just like the treasure cave guarded by snakes and scorpions at its gate.

10. Money versus family: a manufactured myth

Questions like “do you want money or family?” turn two beautiful things into enemies. Amar Chaudhary says to replace “OR” with “AND”: money and family, love and money, can exist together. Family is not an obstacle on your path; it is your strength.

11. The market sells fear and dreams

In response to Sarvesh Mishra’s question, Amar Chaudhary says that only two things really sell: fear and dreams. A home loan ad sells the dream of a home, a deodorant ad sells attraction, and insurance sells fear. Chasing false dreams, he says, is what makes people unhappy.

He also says that, in place of community support, the market built a system of paying for services, which created profit but weakened relationships. He questions social media lines like “relationships don’t matter” and reminds us that in India, hospital bills, weddings and houses have long depended on the help of relatives and friends.

12. Your words are your God

Amar Chaudhary’s central idea is that thoughts are not in our hands, but words are. We can’t stop negative thoughts from arriving, but we can stop negative words from leaving our mouths. To turn the car (your thoughts), turn the steering wheel (your words). Or, to change the channel (thoughts) you use the remote (words).

  • Be careful with “I am …”. What follows “I am” starts to feel permanent, as in “I am sick”, “I am poor”, “I am sad”.
  • Say “going through”. “I am going through a fever” or “I am suffering from fever” makes the problem temporary, something that will pass.
  • Speak what you want to become. In a shop, too, we ask for what we don’t have at home.
  • He reads the Hindi word for God, “Ishwar”, as “Ish + Swar”: your pure and powerful words are your God.

Citing the law of inertia, he says change comes through an external force, so he calls “change comes from inside” an incomplete idea. He says to start with words, and your thoughts and outlook will follow. This is his own view, drawn from his training experience, and it’s open to discussion.

13. Practical ways to fix your thinking

  1. Meditation and pausing. The mind that created the problem can’t solve it, so you have to make it stronger.
  2. A 10-minute self-assessment every day. In the morning or before bed, ask yourself: Did today’s anger really deserve this much anger? Did I give my best? If someone treated me the way I treat others, how would I feel? Is my input proportionate to what I want?
  3. A word audit. Review your conversations from morning to night, remove negative words and add powerful ones.

He illustrates this with the story of the gardener who calls his own employer to check whether she is happy with his work. The lesson: before complaining about the world, your company or society, ask what you have given to the world.

He also makes a sharp observation about anger: our anger is very “intelligent”, because it only goes off at the weak and never at those more powerful. And saying “my attitude depends on how others behave” means handing your control to other people.

14. Who am I? Your contribution is your identity

For the question “who am I?”, Amar Chaudhary finds a simple answer in contribution. Most professions are named after the one who gives: teacher, doctor, confectioner, lawyer. Only the scrap dealer (kabadi) is named after the one who takes. Our identity is what we have given to the world, so ask what your contribution to society is. Names like Kalam, Subhas Chandra Bose and Bhagat Singh are remembered for this reason.

15. The IAS formula for choosing a career

Finally, he shares his formula for choosing a career, while also saying it’s good to meet a professional career counsellor and take tests:

  • I – Interest: What do you enjoy doing?
  • A – Ability: Do you actually have the ability for it, or is the interest just a way of avoiding studies?
  • S – Scope: Does the industry offer room to grow and earn? He says hard work can grow crops even on barren land, but it is smarter to look for fertile land.

He also uses the example of keys and locks: every child is a key, and we try them in the wrong locks and then say the key is faulty. The real job is finding the right lock. This is a guest framework, not a replacement for counselling. Related: Sunil Chopra on quitting a job to start a business and N.P. Singh on sarkari naukri and education.

Closing

In Sarvesh Mishra’s words, the essence of this conversation is that improving a few small things in our lives, especially our words, can make the road ahead much better. At the guest’s request, a second part is also planned. If a later episode is published, it will have its own URL.

Frequently asked questions

Do you need to be successful to be happy?

According to Amar Chaudhary, no. Success comes through effort and time, while happiness is a decision you can make now. That’s why the two shouldn’t be wired to the same switch.

How does changing your words change your life?

Thoughts aren’t in our control, but words are. Just as a remote changes the TV channel, speaking positive and powerful words gradually changes your thinking and outlook, in his view.

What are the key qualities of a good leader?

Taking responsibility, sharing the credit and listening carefully to people.

What is the IAS formula for choosing a career?

Interest, Ability and Scope: identify your interest, test your ability, and finally check the scope of the field. He also recommends a professional career counsellor.

What is the best way to gain clarity of mind?

Meditation and a 10-minute daily self-assessment, in which you question your behaviour, anger and effort.
