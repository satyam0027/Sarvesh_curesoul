Leadership Lessons From the Indian Air Force: What Air Commodore Bahl Says

A soldier does not clock out. That idea runs through this episode. On The Sarvesh Mishra Show, Sarvesh Mishra, who covered defence as a journalist, speaks with Air Commodore Asminder Singh Bahl, a retired Indian Air Force officer. The talk covers the air force today, how fighter pilots think, and what civilians and companies can learn from military leadership.

Why the flag means more to a soldier

The guest says civilians love the flag too, and fly it proudly on 26 January and 15 August. A soldier, he says, carries it every day of his life, whether serving or retired, as a promise to protect the country’s security, integrity and honour. He recalls seeing the Indian flag every morning at the High Commission in Dhaka and feeling that this was his land.

The Indian Air Force today: ground reality

TopicWhat he says
RankingHe says a world directory ranks the IAF third in the world on professionalism, though fourth by size. Please verify the source.
SquadronsAbout 29 combat squadrons now, down from earlier levels, with a push on quality. He says theatre commands would need around 60.
Tejas (LCA)A third production line has opened in Nashik, with Bengaluru’s two. He expects the first batch of jets around March 2026, rising later to about 24 a year.
EnginesIndia depends on the US-made GE F404 engine, and he says deliveries have been slow. A domestic engine effort with a foreign partner is planned.
Next jetsThe fifth-generation AMCA is expected around 2035, so he supports buying about 114 jets from abroad to fill the gap.
RadarHe mentions a made-in-India radar under development, to be tested around 2028.
Ageing fleetJaguars, Mirage 2000s and MiG-29s are 35 to 40 years old, with spare parts a problem. India bought 20 used Jaguars from Oman for parts.

On crashes: he says every pilot loss hurts, because a jet can be replaced and a trained pilot cannot. He says the accident rate has come down over the years but is still too high, and that many crashes involve older aircraft. He declined to speculate on the Tejas crash at the Dubai Air Show because an inquiry was ongoing.

How pilots handle fear

The host asked what runs through a pilot’s mind. The guest’s answer: attention leaves no room for fear. Before engine start there are checks, then oil pressure, RPM and throttle, then emergencies on take-off. The whole sortie is spent in concentration.

He contrasts this with radicalisation, where someone is promised heaven for dying. A soldier, he says, is inducted with a pledge, training and a psychological orientation of nation first, then the people under him, then himself. He quotes a principle he saw at the National Defence Academy: the safety, honour and welfare of your country come first, then the people under you, and your own comfort last.

Lessons from Kargil

He lists what the Kargil Review Committee highlighted and what changed.

Gap in 1999What has improved (his view)
Intelligence failureGreater focus on surveillance and early warning
High-altitude warfareNew platforms such as the Prachand light combat helicopter, which can operate from about 5,000 metres
No night or precision attackLaser-guided precision and night-strike capability in most jets
Poor integration between servicesMoves towards theatre commands

On theatre commands, he suggests a blend: army theatres at operational level and an air force command at strategic level, because the air force has fewer jets than five full theatres would need.

Leader vs manager

ManagerLeader
ApproachImproves existing processesFinds new paths
Sees people asResources to get the most fromHuman beings to inspire
Gets results byTargets and incentivesPassion and example

His definition of a leader: someone others choose to follow to a place they could not reach alone. He adds that civilian systems motivate with pay, incentives and posts, while military leaders inspire by sharing risk.

Lead from the front: two true stories

Sam Manekshaw: as a young lieutenant in World War II, he led his company up a hill, was badly wounded by machine-gun fire, and was carried to a doctor by his orderly. When asked what happened, he joked that he was “kicked by a mule.” The doctor, impressed by his spirit, treated him, and he later became Chief of Army Staff and Field Marshal.

The guest’s own experience: in 1998, as a flight safety officer, he saw a fighter crash on landing and slide into parked aircraft, setting three jets on fire. He says he jumped into the flames to reach a trapped airman, and that his example brought others in to help control the fire. He says the point is not heroism, but that people join you when you take the same risks.

Military vs civilian work culture

  • Ownership: he says he treats every organisation he joins as his own.
  • Credit: military leaders say “my team did it,” while self-promotion is common elsewhere.
  • Decision making: he claims civil systems form committees to avoid signing, while military officers lead from the front. He says most of India’s Param Vir Chakra recipients were officers, citing 12 of 21. Please verify this figure.
  • Contentment: he does not own a bungalow, still pays a home-loan EMI and says peace and satisfaction come from gratitude, not greed.

Three changes for any organisation

  1. A clear vision or common purpose each year.
  2. Discipline and punctuality, including keeping commitments to yourself and others.
  3. Synergy through teamwork, working together so output is larger than the sum of individual efforts.

His example: as a director, he says 22,000 people were trained across 38 districts in Bihar in a year, with most passing, by bringing doctors, hospitals and training centres into one plan.

NCC, traffic and veterans

NCC: he suggests making at least the basic NCC certificate compulsory in schools and colleges, using veterans as trainers. It teaches discipline, self-protection and service.

Traffic: he says about 1.72 lakh people died in road accidents in 2023, around 20 an hour. He believes strict traffic discipline would build discipline everywhere. Please check the latest official figure.

Veterans: he asks policy makers to place retired senior officers where they can shape young minds and a new work culture.

A line from history: he points to Genghis Khan’s empire to argue for merit over loyalty in appointments, which is a view to debate, not a historical consensus.

Practical lessons for you

  • Put the mission first, then your team, then yourself.
  • Lead by example. Do the hard task before you ask others to.
  • Share credit, and own mistakes.
  • Keep your commitments and be on time.
  • Stay physically fit. The guest says he runs several days a week and trains at the gym, and that fitness builds mental resilience.
  • Act with others. Aim for teamwork, not solo heroics.
Key takeaways
  • A soldier carries the flag as a lifelong commitment, not a job.
  • Focus and training leave no room for fear in the cockpit.
  • The IAF is stretched on squadron strength and ageing aircraft, and plans to add jets. Treat those numbers as guest claims.
  • Kargil led to better intelligence, precision strike and integration efforts, in his view.
  • Leaders inspire, managers optimise.
  • Lead from the front, share credit and keep your commitments.
  • Vision, discipline and synergy can improve any organisation.

Conclusion

The strongest lesson from Air Commodore Bahl is simple: leadership is shown by what you do, not by your title. Put the purpose first, take the same risks you ask of others, share the credit and keep your word. You do not need to wear a uniform to apply these ideas in your family, team or company.

Frequently asked questions

What leadership lessons come from the Indian Air Force?

Nation or mission first, lead from the front, discipline, teamwork and sharing credit.

Who is the guest?

Air Commodore Asminder Singh Bahl, a retired IAF officer introduced with the Vayu Sena Medal for gallantry. Please confirm the spelling and awards with him.

How do fighter pilots handle fear?

By concentration. Checks, procedures and focus on the task leave little room for fear, he says.

How many squadrons does the IAF have?

He says about 29 combat squadrons. The commonly cited sanctioned strength is higher. Please check the official figure.

What is the difference between a leader and a manager?

A manager improves existing processes, while a leader finds new paths and inspires people.

What did Kargil teach the forces?

The need for better intelligence, high-altitude capability, precision and night strike, and integration between services.

Why does he want NCC to be compulsory?

He believes it builds discipline, self-protection skills and service values at scale.

What are the three changes he suggests for organisations?

Clear vision, discipline and synergy through teamwork.

How can civilians apply military discipline?

Keep commitments, be punctual, stay fit and put purpose before personal comfort.
