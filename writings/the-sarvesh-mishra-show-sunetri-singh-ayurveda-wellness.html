Ayurveda: इलाज या धंधा? How a Vaidya Says You Can Build a Wellness Business From ₹0

Why This Ayurveda Conversation Matters

Someone flies to Rishikesh, learns yoga and Ayurveda, goes back to California and teaches it for good money. The country where it all began is still working out how to cash in. Why?

That was the starting question when Sarvesh Mishra sat down with Dr. Sunetri Singh. Her answer: Ayurveda was never a poor man's science. Vaidyas served kings, and gold and diamond ash preparations belong to the tradition. Then cheap chemical pills arrived, and people forgot its value. Who Is Dr. Sunetri Singh?

She calls herself a nadi vaidya, meaning she reads the pulse (nadi pariksha) to see which dosha and tissue are out of balance. She holds a BAMS and an MD in Ayurveda (Panchakarma), says she has practised since 2010, and runs an Ayurveda and Panchkarma centre in Noida along with a training institute.

She says the centre is NABH accredited and empanelled with CGHS, and that the institute's certificate courses run under bodies linked to the Ministry of AYUSH. These are her own claims from the episode, so verify them before you book or enrol.

Ayurveda Career Options After BAMS

  • Clinical practice. Many towns still lack a vaidya people trust.
  • Online Ayurveda consultation. She says around 200 countries recognise Ayurveda, which opens up patients abroad.
  • Teaching at an Ayurveda college after an MD.
  • Ayurvedic products, through a small manufacturing unit or third-party manufacturing.
  • Exams for practising abroad, such as Dubai.
  • Certificate courses, like Ayurveda diet consultant or Panchakarma technician, for graduates who can't open a clinic.

Not every role needs a BAMS. Related: Yogacharya Dhakaram on yoga as a career and N.P. Singh on sarkari naukri and education.

How to Start an Ayurvedic Products Business Without a Factory

Don't build your own plant at first. Use third-party manufacturing, order batches of 100, 200 or 500, and test the market. Formulas are open, but quality depends on proper ingredients in classical proportions.

Her chyawanprash test: price the herbs, count the ingredients, add the sugar, and ask whether the shelf price is even possible. She says the same about very cheap ghee. Related: Lalit Arora on building a brand without loans and investors.

Root Cause, Panchakarma and Prevention

She explains that Ayurveda tries to remove the cause and treat the affected tissue instead of only the symptom. She reports good outcomes in neurological problems, arthritis, ligament injuries and some spine conditions, but says results vary and she doesn't promise cures. This is her clinical view, not medical advice. Related: Dr. Sumedh Sandanshiv on when knee surgery is really necessary.

Her Daily Habits

Two meals a day, sleeping on time, homemade uptan and shampoo instead of soap, and a weekly junk food treat served in a bowl rather than eaten from the packet. She also suggests a three-second pause before acting and holding water in your mouth to settle anxiety. Treat these as her suggestions, and see a doctor for serious symptoms.

Wellness Tourism in India and the Robot Question

She expects wellness tourism to outgrow medical tourism as people focus on prevention. Can robots replace therapists? A machine can massage, she says, but it can't give the warmth of a healer telling you you'll be fine. Related: Sanjeev Jain on AI, jobs and the future of work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Ayurveda a good career in India?
According to Dr. Sunetri, yes, through clinical practice, teaching, products, certificate courses and online consultations.
Can I start a wellness business with ₹1 lakh?
She suggests renting a therapy room in an existing resort and collaborating with two trained therapists. Results vary.
Do I need a BAMS to work in Ayurveda?
Not for every role. She mentions Ayurveda diet consultant and Panchakarma technician courses.
What can I do after BAMS besides opening a clinic?
Teach after an MD, build a products business, offer online consultations, or take exams to practise abroad.
Which Ayurveda business earns the most?
She says digital content suits zero investment, products and institutes scale later, and retreats need reputation first.
Do I need a factory to sell Ayurvedic products?
No. She advises third-party manufacturing and test batches of 100 to 500.
How long does an Ayurveda business take to grow?
About three years, with setbacks.
Is Ayurveda expensive?
She argues it can cost far less than long hospital stays, though it depends on the treatment.
Will AI or robots replace Ayurveda practitioners?
She believes human warmth and trust can't be replaced.
Will wellness tourism outgrow medical tourism?
She expects so, as prevention gets more attention.

Conclusion

The conversation between Sarvesh Mishra and Dr. Sunetri Singh treats Ayurveda as both a clinical tradition and a possible livelihood. The practical thread is to start within the budget you actually have, verify claims before you enrol or invest, and treat earnings figures as illustrations rather than guarantees.
