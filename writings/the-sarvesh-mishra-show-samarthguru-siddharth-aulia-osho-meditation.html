What Is Osho Meditation? Dynamic Meditation, Awareness and Enlightenment

Guest: Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia, founder of Samarthguru Dhara
Host: Sarvesh Mishra · Sarvesh Mishra Show

How Many Oshos Are There Today?

The episode opens with a sharp line. There are countless versions of Osho walking around today, and many of them contradict each other. Some grow beards, some grow moustaches, some criticise the world in Osho's name while forgetting that Osho himself never asked anyone to imitate him. On this episode of The Sarvesh Mishra Show, host Sarvesh Mishra sits with Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia to separate real Osho meditation from the noise around it, and to ask why a teacher who was once misunderstood is now read as scripture by an entire generation. Who Is Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia?

Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia is the founder of Samarthguru Dhara, earlier known as Oshodhara, one of the largest meditation movements built directly on Osho's methods. He completed his schooling at Netarhat Residential School and later earned an M.Sc. and Ph.D. from IIT (Indian School of Mines), Dhanbad, followed by a senior executive management course at Manchester Business School in the UK. He worked as a lecturer and later served as General Manager at Coal India Limited until 2002, before turning fully to spiritual work. His path blends Gyan Yoga, Bhakti Yoga and Karma Yoga into what he calls Sahaj Yoga, a natural and balanced approach to awakening.

His main ashram, Samarthguru Dham, sits near Murthal, close to Sonipat, about fifty kilometres from Delhi. Seekers from across India travel there for meditation camps and spiritual programs that trace their roots back to Osho's original teaching. Why Osho Faces So Much Criticism

The conversation begins with a question many people carry quietly. If Osho spoke about truth and awareness, why does he still attract so much criticism decades later? Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia explains that meditation itself has become theatre in many hands. People adopt Osho's appearance, his robes, his style of speech, without touching the actual inner work he described. Osho criticised everyone equally, every religion, every ritual, every guru, and today many of his own followers repeat that same criticism without his depth or his awareness. The guest is direct about this. Anyone who is only copying Osho's methods without genuine inner transformation, he says, is like a washerman's dog that belongs to no house and reaches nowhere.

Osho on Money and Meditation

One of the most talked-about ideas in the episode is Osho's relationship with wealth. Critics often say Osho's teachings became commercial, that his methods turned into a business. Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia reframes this completely. Osho never rejected money. He said clearly that earning wealth is one of the greatest supports for real sadhana, because a person who is anxious about survival cannot sit quietly for inner work. Osho gave the world a single word for this balance: Zorba the Buddha. Zorba stands for a person rich in the material world, and Buddha stands for a person rich in awareness. Osho's real teaching was never renunciation of wealth. It was freedom from attachment to wealth while living fully and earning honestly.

Love, Attachment and the Difference Between Them

The episode spends real time untangling love from attachment, a distinction that Osho repeated often and that critics frequently misread. According to the discussion, people confuse closeness with love and label physical intimacy as vasana, or lust, when in truth vasana has nothing to do with genuine love. Attachment breeds boredom and eventual disrespect, while real love, in Osho's framing, keeps a quiet distance of respect even inside deep closeness. This is one of the ideas that drew the harshest criticism toward Osho during his life, since people assumed his talk of closeness to another human being was somehow opposed to reaching the divine. Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia argues that this reading missed Osho's actual point entirely.

Destiny, Free Will and Prarabdha

The conversation also turns philosophical around fate and Prarabdha, the idea of destiny already written. Is everything predetermined, or do humans write their own path through daily choices? The guest offers a balanced middle view here. Prarabdha exists, but so does purushartha, human effort. A person who commits an offence today is, in effect, writing tomorrow's consequence with their own hand right now. Destiny and self-written choice work together rather than one erasing the other.

Why Osho Is More Relevant Today Than When He Was Alive

A large part of the episode explores a genuine puzzle. Osho spoke to full houses during his life, yet was widely misunderstood by mainstream society. Today, decades later, young people between twelve and forty-five years old are turning to him more than ever. Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia compares this to the story of Faraday and Edison. Faraday discovered electricity as pure science, but the world could not use it directly. Edison later built the bulb from that same science, and suddenly everyone could see the light. Osho, in this analogy, did Faraday's work. Teachers who came after him, speaking in simpler, more grounded language, are doing Edison's work, turning Osho's science of awareness into something ordinary people can actually practise.

Before Osho, spirituality was mostly tied to scriptures, penance and years spent in Himalayan caves, with no clear scientific grounding. Osho was the first to place meditation on a scientific footing, showing that awakening does not require running away from the world. It requires waking up exactly where a person already stands.

The Urban Sannyasi Path

Perhaps the most practical idea in the episode is the Urban Sannyasi, a term the host and guest both use for a householder who lives fully in the world while staying inwardly awake. There is no need to leave family, change clothes, or retreat into a forest. Earn well, dress well, eat well, the discussion says, but do not let these things become the goal of life. Keep them as tools and nothing more. This is presented as a direct extension of Osho's Zorba the Buddha idea, made practical for modern Indian life where most people cannot and should not abandon their responsibilities to chase enlightenment. The Four Goals of Life

Near the end, Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia lays out a framework for anyone who feels lost, especially young people searching for direction. He names four goals rooted in Indian tradition: artha, dharma, kama and moksha. Artha means earning a livelihood. Dharma means using that wealth for the good of others, not hoarding it selfishly. Kama here refers to responsibility, particularly toward family, which the guest carefully separates from duty imposed by others. Moksha is freedom from constant craving, understanding the difference between a wish, which carries no expectation, and a craving, which quietly expects the world to fulfil it. A person who lives all four goals together, he says, is a truly successful person, and success itself is finally defined as living in anand, or genuine inner bliss.

Practical Guide: How to Begin With This Approach

  1. Start where you are. Sit quietly for ten minutes a day at home rather than waiting for a retreat or a Himalayan trip.
  2. Separate earning from craving. Keep working toward money and comfort, but watch the moment desire turns into constant demand.
  3. Practise awareness in ordinary tasks, while eating, walking or working, instead of treating meditation as a separate hour cut off from daily life.
  4. Read or listen to original Osho talks directly at osho.com before accepting any secondhand interpretation of his words.
  5. Choose one teacher or one method and stay with it for a few months rather than sampling many paths at once, since depth needs continuity.
  6. Keep family and work responsibilities intact. The Urban Sannyasi path never asks a person to withdraw from duty.
  7. Revisit the same talk or episode more than once. As this episode itself notes, spiritual ideas open up new meaning on a second and third listen.

Benefits Discussed in the Episode

  • A calmer relationship with money, since wealth is treated as a support for sadhana rather than something to renounce or feel guilty about.
  • Healthier relationships, once love is separated from attachment and expectation.
  • Less internal conflict between ambition and spirituality, because the Zorba the Buddha model allows both to exist together.
  • A clear personal framework through the four goals of life that reduces confusion about life direction.
  • Freedom from the pressure to renounce family, career or normal life in order to pursue inner growth.
  • A more grounded, practical entry point into meditation for people who found scripture-heavy classical spirituality difficult to relate to.

Common Mistakes People Make

  • Copying Osho's outer style, clothing, speech or mannerisms while skipping the actual inner practice of awareness.
  • Assuming criticism of rigid religion means rejecting all discipline, ending up scattered instead of consistent.
  • Treating wealth as either the whole goal of life or as something spiritually impure, both of which miss the Zorba the Buddha balance.
  • Confusing physical closeness or intimacy with love, then feeling guilty or judged for ordinary human relationships.
  • Mistaking imagination for intuition, acting on a passing thought as if it were deep inner guidance.
  • Postponing inner work indefinitely with the idea that spirituality can wait until wealth, career or age arrive at some future point.

Key Takeaways

  1. Osho criticised every rigid system, and many followers now repeat his criticism without living his actual awareness.
  2. Osho never opposed wealth. His idea of Zorba the Buddha combines material success with inner meditation.
  3. Real love keeps respect and distance alive, while attachment slowly breeds boredom and disrespect.
  4. Destiny and personal effort work together rather than one cancelling the other.
  5. Osho gave spirituality a scientific structure, replacing blind penance with clear, practical meditation methods.
  6. The Urban Sannyasi model allows a person to stay fully engaged with career and family while pursuing inner awakening.
  7. A balanced life across artha, dharma, kama and moksha is presented as the real definition of a successful life.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia?
He is the founder of Samarthguru Dhara, formerly Oshodhara, a direct disciple of Osho and an enlightened teacher based at an ashram near Murthal, Sonipat.
What is Osho meditation actually about?
It is a scientific approach to awareness that does not require leaving the world, renouncing family, or practising harsh penance. It asks a person to wake up exactly where they already are.
Did Osho support earning money?
Yes. Osho called wealth one of the biggest supports for genuine meditation and gave the world the idea of Zorba the Buddha, living richly in the world while staying rich in awareness.
What is the difference between love and attachment in Osho's teaching?
Love keeps a quiet respect even in closeness, while attachment turns familiarity into boredom and eventual disrespect.
What does Urban Sannyasi mean?
It describes a householder who earns, dresses and lives normally in the world while staying inwardly detached and awake, without becoming a traditional renunciate.
Is everything in life predetermined by Prarabdha?
The episode presents a middle view. Destiny exists, but personal effort and daily choices also shape what happens next.
Why is Osho more popular now than during his lifetime?
Because teachers who came after him translated his high-level teaching into simpler, everyday language that a wider audience can actually apply.
What are the four goals of life mentioned in this episode?
Artha, earning a livelihood; dharma, using wealth for the good of others; kama, fulfilling family responsibility; and moksha, freedom from constant craving.

Osho remains one of the most quoted and most misunderstood teachers of modern India. This conversation between Sarvesh Mishra and Samarthguru Siddharth Aulia does not try to defend every criticism aimed at Osho. Instead it separates genuine meditation from imitation, and shows why ideas once considered radical — earning wealth without guilt, loving without possessiveness, staying in the world instead of fleeing it — now feel closer to common sense for a generation raised on constant noise and comparison. The real invitation here is simple. Begin where you stand, stay honest about the difference between imagination and intuition, and treat every responsibility in life as part of the path rather than an obstacle to it.
