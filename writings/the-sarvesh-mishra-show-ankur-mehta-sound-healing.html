Can life’s problems really be addressed through sound and vibration? Is everything already written by destiny, or do our actions shape our future? On The Sarvesh Mishra Show, Sarvesh Mishra sits with sound healer and spiritual practitioner Ankur Mehta for a long conversation on those questions. This blog summarises that discussion.

Light, heat and sound: three fundamental energies

Ankur Mehta explains that creation can be understood through three energies. He sees Vishnu as the form of light energy that sustains life, Brahma as heat energy (where there is creation there is warmth, from setting curd to a mother’s womb), and Shiva as nada, or sound energy, from whose damaru the notes emerged. What science calls the “humming sound of the universe”, he says, the rishis called the sound of Om.

Sound and science: the effect of vibration

The conversation touched on cymatics, where sand particles on a plate form geometric patterns when exposed to sound frequencies, and on ultrasound in physiotherapy, which is used to relax muscles. Ankur Mehta argues that because the body contains a large proportion of water, and sound travels much faster in water than in air, the effect of sound reaches every cell. Treat that as his illustration, not a clinical claim.

He also stresses that Indian classical music is not mere entertainment but a discipline, with different ragas prescribed for different times and states. He recounted the well-known story of Tansen and the ragas Deepak and Malhar in this context.

Temple bells: an ancient sound bath

Ankur Mehta regards the many bells in a temple as a form of sound healing. By the time a devotee reaches the sanctum, he says, the sound of the bells has calmed the mind enough to receive guidance. Drums, manjira, bhajans and kirtan, in his view, belong to the same tradition.

IQ, EQ and SQ: the road to sound healing

Answering “why sound healing?”, Ankur Mehta described three human faculties: IQ (intellectual problem solving), EQ (understanding one’s own and others’ emotions) and SQ, or spiritual quotient (the capacity to ask “Who am I?” and “What is liberation?”). He says this third faculty is what sets humans apart, and that nada works not only on the body and mind but at the level of the soul.

He and his wife Manjari left their corporate careers to spend eight and a half years living an ashram life in Haridwar, where the writings of his guru introduced him to Nada Brahma and its effect on the chakras. He has worked with sound healing since around 2013 and is today the founder of Pause N Learn.

Three simple practices: Bhramari, Mool Bandha and Jalandhar Bandha

The steps below are how the guest described them on the show. They are not a class, and they are not medical treatment. If you have heart, blood-pressure, pregnancy, pelvic or throat conditions, ask a doctor and a trained teacher before trying bandhas or breath holds.

1. Bhramari pranayama (humming)

Referring to vagus nerve stimulation, Ankur Mehta said humming can stimulate this nerve. In his words, closing the ears is not mandatory for Bhramari: it can be done in a quiet spot, or even at a traffic light with eyes and ears open. He also noted that while humming, thoughts naturally slow down.

2. Jalandhar Bandha

Inhale, lower the chin and apply a lock at the throat, hold as long as is comfortable, then exhale. Ankur Mehta links this to the Vishuddhi (throat) chakra and the space element, and says suppressed, unexpressed emotions in the throat region can be related to thyroid issues. That thyroid link is his view, not a diagnosis.

3. Mool Bandha

Contract and hold the anal and perineal muscles. He cited Swami Sivananda’s book on the subject, which calls it a “master key” of practice. His guru’s advice was to do it up to 101 times a day, and no more. Do not treat that number as a medical dose.

The “mini capsule” approach

Mool Bandha on waking, Bhramari while walking or at a traffic signal, and Jalandhar Bandha whenever you have five minutes. They can be done together or separately. Ankur Mehta advises starting with whichever one interests you rather than taking on everything at once.

4. Pran Akarshan Pranayama

A very simple practice, as he describes it: take one deep breath slowly, release it slowly through the mouth, don’t count, and bring to mind your guru, God or centre of energy. It can be done before a meeting or exam, or lying in bed at night. According to Ankur Mehta, a few weeks of regular practice can improve sleep quality. Sleep claims are guest experience, not a guarantee.

Tibetan bowls, the shankh and chakra frequencies

In Ankur Mehta’s experience, the most effective instruments have been Tibetan singing bowls, especially eight-metal (ashtadhatu) ones, where the proportion of metals determines sound quality. He cautioned that the market sells five-, six- and eight-metal bowls, and that ornamental bowls are sometimes passed off as ashtadhatu at high prices. He described the conch (shankh) as a sound healing instrument made by nature itself.

In his framework every chakra has its own frequency. For example, for the Muladhara (root) chakra, linked to the earth element, grounding and fear, a bowl matching that frequency is used. He considers emotional mapping, listening to a person’s childhood and grievances, an essential part of the process.

The sound healing market and misconceptions

Sarvesh Mishra asked whether a large market has grown around sound healing, confusing many people. Ankur Mehta agreed that whenever a discipline becomes popular, dilution follows, just as yoga became “yoga” and “hot yoga”. He also expressed concern about people who take a basic course and consider themselves healers. For those who can’t afford bowls or sessions, he suggested starting at home with humming, chanting Om and a five-minute daily family ritual. He added that a recording on a phone does not carry the same physical vibration as a live instrument.

Why doesn’t the mind heal? The Gita’s answer

Drawing on Gita chapters 2 and 3, Ankur Mehta described the sequence of the mind’s decline: dwelling on objects, then desire, then anger when desire is unmet, then delusion, loss of memory and loss of discernment. In his view we do wrong despite knowing better because of ingrained habits (sanskaras), and the biggest cause is postponing responsibility toward the soul (pramada, or procrastination).

As a remedy he offered the practice of “Koham to Soham”: when anger or restlessness arises, ask “Who is angry?” and attend to the sounds “so” and “ham” in the breath. Through Ramakrishna Paramhansa’s story of the merchant and the servant, he explained that the mind must be kept as a servant and never allowed to become the master.

Destiny vs karma: is everything pre-decided?

Near the end, Sarvesh Mishra asked the question many viewers carry: does life run on destiny or on karma? Ankur Mehta replied that these are two different truths, not contradictory ones.

  • Sanchit karma: the stored account of all actions across one’s journey.
  • Prarabdh: the portion of Sanchit to be experienced in this life. It has to be lived through.
  • Kriyamana karma: what we are doing now. This is where our freedom lies, and it becomes the seed of the future.

Using the image of a field and seeds, he said the law is “as you sow, so you reap”, while which seed to sow remains our choice. He reconciled Guru Vashishtha’s “Bhavi prabal” with Tulsidas’s “Karma pradhan vishwa kari rakha” in this light. When this is understood, he said, the complaint “why me?” gives way to responsibility and discernment, for which satsang and the guru’s grace matter.

One can practise spirituality while living as a householder; going to the Himalayas is not necessary. (The core idea behind The Urban Sannyasi series — a different channel from this Show episode.)

About the guest

According to the brief, Ankur Mehta is a gold-medalist MBA graduate, sound healing practitioner, trainer and founder of Pause N Learn. His areas of work and interest include sound healing, Tibetan singing bowls, chakras, energy awareness and the connection between ancient Indian wisdom and modern scientific understanding.

After teaching MBA students, Ankur and his wife, Manjari (an ex-IBM professional), left their corporate careers to spend eight and a half years living an ashram life in Haridwar. Their journey eventually led to the creation of Pause N Learn, through which they offer sound healing trainings, spiritual retreats, garbh sanskar, conscious parenting, meditation and corporate wellness programs. Garbh sanskar work mentioned here is his offering, not the Show episode with Dr. Vishnu Mane.

Frequently asked questions

What is sound healing?

According to Ankur Mehta, it is the practice of delivering sound energy to the body and mind in a correct, regulated way through an instrument or the voice, where sound is linked to a chakra, note and element. Simply playing a pleasant sound is not sound healing, in his view.

How do you practise Bhramari pranayama?

Take a deep breath and hum like a bee as you exhale. Closing the ears is not necessary, he says; it can be done in a quiet place or at a traffic signal. Guest description, not medical advice.

What are Mool Bandha and Jalandhar Bandha?

Mool Bandha is holding the anal and perineal muscles; Jalandhar Bandha is inhaling, lowering the chin and locking the throat. Learn from a qualified teacher.

What is Pran Akarshan Pranayama?

A slow, deep-breathing practice done while remembering your guru or source of energy, recommended in the episode for stress and sleep. Guest view, not a treatment.

What is the difference between Prarabdh, Sanchit and Kriyamana karma?

Sanchit is the stored account of karma, Prarabdh is the portion experienced in this life, and Kriyamana is what we do now.
