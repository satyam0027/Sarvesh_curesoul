Most of us have said it at least once: I’ll start saving when my salary goes up. Then the salary goes up, and somehow the money is still gone by the 20th. On The Sarvesh Mishra Show, Sarvesh Mishra sits with wealth coach Sandeep Gupta on habits before returns.

Here’s a number worth thinking about. India now has close to 10 crore SIP accounts, yet in October 2026 about 59 lakh SIPs were stopped or completed, against roughly 51 lakh new ones. Plenty of people are starting, and plenty are quitting. The difference usually isn’t income. It’s habit.

The short version: if you want to know how to save money from your salary and build wealth over time, work on habits before returns. Pay yourself first, make your savings invisible, track where the money goes, raise your SIP every time you get a raise, keep loans light, keep learning, and give it time.

Why your habits matter more than your salary

Sandeep Gupta started his career in 2007 on a salary of around Rs 7,000 to Rs 7,500. He didn’t save much at first, only about Rs 1,500 a month. What he stuck to was the routine. Years later, that routine and the skills he built alongside it are what he credits for his move from a banking job into coaching.

We went through the whole conversation and pulled out nine habits you can start this week. Where the podcast skipped something important, like inflation or an emergency fund, the Word brief added it.

9 money saving habits for salaried people in India

1. Pay yourself first

Decide how much you’ll save before you decide how much you’ll spend. Sandeep suggests keeping at least 10 to 15% of your income aside and treating it as money you never touch. The easiest way is a standing instruction that moves the money the day after your salary arrives. If it waits for you to remember, it usually won’t happen.

2. Make your savings invisible

UPI made spending painless, which is great until you check your balance on the 18th. Sandeep’s point is that many people’s biggest savings sit in PF or PPF for one simple reason: they never saw that money. Do the same on purpose. Set up an auto SIP or a recurring deposit, and mentally treat your salary as a smaller number than it is.

3. Track every rupee for 30 days

He says he has written down his expenses for more than twenty years. You don’t need an app; a notebook works fine. Do it for 30 days and don’t judge yourself, just look. Many people find two or three small leaks that add up to more than they expected.

4. Start small and stay consistent

Can’t manage Rs 500 yet? Start with Rs 100, or even a few rupees a day. Sandeep’s argument is that tiny deposits build confidence and self control, and that matters more than the amount at the start. In the first month, the only goal is to prove to yourself that you can do it.

5. Let lifestyle grow slower than income

When the salary goes up, spending usually goes up with it. Try splitting every raise instead: part for your lifestyle, part for your SIP. Do the same with bonuses. A simple step up of 10% in your SIP every year can change the final number a lot.

6. Avoid loans taken for status

In his view, most loans are lifestyle choices: a car, a phone, a holiday, or a big EMI taken to impress others. When EMIs eat 30 to 40% of your salary, you lose the freedom to take risks, switch jobs or start something of your own. Before you buy a house, compare the EMI with the rent for a similar one. If a new phone or car strains your budget, a cheaper or used one is perfectly fine. A loan for a genuine need can still be sensible.

7. Invest in skills in small monthly doses

Spending Rs 300 on a book or a low cost course every month is a small price for a skill that can raise your income. A higher income only helps if you save part of it, so pair this habit with the first one.

8. Choose the right circle

Sandeep recommends spending time with people who think bigger than you do. That doesn’t mean dropping old friends. Add a few new voices through podcasts, books and mentors, and spend less time in conversations that keep you stuck in complaints.

9. Stay patient

He recalls the Hindi saying about a gardener who waters a plant a hundred pots at a time, yet the fruit still comes only in its own season. Wealth works the same way. Checking your portfolio every day won’t speed it up.

Money mistakes and behaviour traps to avoid

  • Present bias: today’s comfort feels bigger than tomorrow’s security. Automation beats willpower.
  • Lifestyle creep: every raise quietly turns into a new fixed cost.
  • Social comparison: buying to keep up with others is a common reason people take EMIs.
  • Panic when markets fall: many investors stop their SIPs at the wrong time. Total mutual fund assets in India fell about 5.1% in September 2026, even as SIP inflows hit a record.
  • Trusting big claims: a multi million dollar business or dozens of income streams are the guest’s own statements. Use them as motivation, and verify before you pay for any program.

Key takeaways
  • Your habits decide your wealth more than your salary does.
  • Save first, spend later, and automate it.
  • Track your spending for 30 days to find the leaks.
  • Let your lifestyle grow slower than your income.
  • Skip status loans, and compare rent with EMI before buying a home.
  • Waiting ten years can cost over a crore in this example.
  • Keep an emergency fund so you never have to stop your SIP.
  • Returns are market linked, so treat every number here as an illustration.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best money habit for a salaried person in India?

Paying yourself first. Move a fixed share of your salary into savings or an SIP before you spend anything, and automate it so you don’t have to remember.

How can I stop wasting money every month?

Track every expense for 30 days and look for the biggest leaks. Then set a monthly limit for those. You don’t have to give up small pleasures, only the spending you don’t really enjoy.

How much should I save if my salary is low?

Start with whatever you can manage, even Rs 100 to Rs 500, and aim for 10 to 15% over time. Raise the amount whenever your income goes up.

Is it too late to start investing at 35?

No. Starting later means investing more or for longer, but it’s still better than waiting. Use the cost of waiting table to plan your amount.

Should I clear loans before investing?

Clear costly debt like credit cards first. For other loans, compare the interest rate with the returns you can reasonably expect, and keep an emergency fund in place.
