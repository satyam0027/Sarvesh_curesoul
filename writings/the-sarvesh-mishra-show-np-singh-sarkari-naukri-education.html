Is Your Degree Really Useless? India's Education System, Explained by a Former IAS Officer

Whose fault is it — the youth, or the system?

Host Sarvesh Mishra opens with a frustration many families know: by 25 or 26, a graduate often finds their degree has become, in his words, “a piece of paper” — while the education system that produced it has not changed.

Dr. N.P. Singh rejects the idea that today's youth are somehow lacking in values or direction. Instead, he places responsibility on the previous two generations — from Baby Boomers through Gen X — who shaped the current system's “vision” without adapting it for a dynamic, fast-changing world. Education, he argues, is meant to socialise a child using a vision that is partly eternal and partly responsive to social, economic and cultural change. When a generation builds children using only its own generation's vision, without updating it, the mismatch shows up years later — exactly when that young adult tries to apply it to a 21st-century reality.

If we leave aside about 10% of cases, where is today's education system actually taking the remaining 90%?
— N.P. Singh, The Sarvesh Mishra Show

Macaulay's ghost — how 1835 still shapes Indian classrooms

The conversation turns to a pointed question: is what India calls “our” education system actually India's own — or is it still Lord Macaulay's? Dr. Singh does not hedge. In 1835, he says, Macaulay's reforms replaced India's Gurukul tradition and, in the process, embedded a single equation into the Indian psyche: education equals a job, and a job means a government job.

He is careful to note that Macaulay likely believed he was serving his own nation's interests. This is not framed as a personal attack, but as a structural critique. The lasting damage, in his telling, is that India's curriculum still teaches history as memorised facts — kings, dates, wars won and lost — without building the competency of understanding why political, economic and social change actually happens. That gap, he argues, produces students trained to memorise for an exam and then forget everything once the exam is over.

A common misconception addressed in the episode is that the current system is authentically “Indian.” Dr. Singh's position is that India's real inheritance is the Gurukul model — and that reviving it does not mean anything sectarian or religious, but a tradition focused on building a complete individual and, through that, a nation.

The reservation debate is a distraction — here is the real math

One of the episode's sharpest arguments concerns reservation. Dr. Singh calls it “a damru handed to the youth” — something to bang on and dance to while the real problem goes unaddressed. He walks through the numbers himself: roughly 30 crore (300 million) young people fall into the productive 21-to-36 age bracket that the system funnels toward government jobs, while total government job openings across state, public-sector and central government roles in a year run to roughly 10–15 lakh (1–1.5 million).

Even a hypothetical 100% reservation for any single community, he argues, would not meaningfully change the outcome — the pool of unemployed youth within that community would still run into the crores. The real question, in his view, is not who gets the shrinking number of government jobs, but why the entire system still funnels 30 crore young people toward a job category that can only absorb a tiny fraction of them in the first place.

From the episode

This isn't a problem of reservation. It's a damru placed in the hands of the youth — play it, dance to it, and keep fighting each other.
— N.P. Singh

He extends this into a broader critique of political manifestos: education, he says, typically ranks near the bottom of party priorities because parties calculate that voters don't reward education promises the way they reward visible short-term giveaways.

Rote learning and the degree that's “divorced” from skill and vision

Returning to the “piece of paper” framing, Dr. Singh names the core defect directly: a degree today is “divorced” from both skill and vision. The assessment system rewards memorisation — a student reproduces what was memorised, scores marks, is declared successful, and then forgets the syllabus entirely. Naturally, he argues, such a system cannot equip anyone to face real-world challenges, so blaming the youth for the outcome makes little sense. That certificate-versus-capability tension also runs through Yogacharya Dhakaram on yoga as a career and Gurnandan Singh on how the brain actually learns.

What is Sanatan Dharma, really? Philosophy vs ritual

The conversation takes a philosophical turn when Sarvesh Mishra asks why so many groups in India feel threatened when the Gurukul tradition is invoked, associating it with a single religion. Dr. Singh's answer distinguishes sharply between theology (belief-based, where practices are built around what one is told to accept on faith) and philosophy (reason- and reflection-based, examining one's relationship to existence, society and the cosmos).

His argument: colonial-era textbook framing categorised Vedantic thought, Buddhist philosophy and Jain philosophy as “religions” akin to Old and New Testament theology — when, in his reading, they are reasoned philosophical traditions built on dialogue and inquiry, not blind belief. He cites the Ishopanishad's opening verse — an instruction to “enjoy through renunciation,” not claim ownership over nature's resources — as a precursor to ideas the world now calls sustainability and minimalism.

His central claim: confusing ritual practice with the underlying philosophy is what fractured Sanatan thought into narrow religious identity — and reclaiming the philosophy, not just the rituals, is what he believes modern education should draw from.

Redesigning the curriculum: vocational tracks, entrepreneurship and dynamic learning

Asked how he would rebuild Indian education “from zero,” Dr. Singh outlines concrete steps already underway at the Bharatiya Shiksha Board:

  • A “knowledge creator and leadership” orientation in every subject and class, replacing the current “job-seeker” orientation
  • A dynamic curriculum — content structured so students learn how to apply and adapt knowledge, not simply memorise it
  • Mandatory vocational training from Class 9 to 12 — roughly 100 periods a year across four years, delivered through India's government Skill Sector Councils (hospitality, green energy, IT, AI assistant/cloud computing, web design and more), offered free through government tie-ups
  • Entrepreneurship education from Class 6, to normalise the idea of building something rather than only seeking a job

He is candid about the resistance: many parents still push back, insisting their child should become an engineer, doctor or IAS officer — worried vocational exposure will “limit” their ambitions. His counter-argument is that the vocational hour does not replace academic subjects; it runs alongside them, meaning even a future engineer or doctor graduates with baseline literacy in AI tools or web design — what he calls a “minimum condition of literacy” for the current era. Convincing schools to adopt this at scale, he admits, still takes considerable effort — only a handful of students per school currently opt in.

His broader appeal: education subsidy — not blanket cash or ration subsidies — should be India's central policy tool, ensuring every child, regardless of economic background, gets access to a baseline quality curriculum that marries vocational and academic learning. Will AI replace traditional jobs in India?

Responding to a viewer question about AI eliminating millions of traditional jobs, Dr. Singh reframes the stakes. Technology, historically, concentrates wealth and creates division before it becomes an equaliser — the Industrial Revolution followed the same pattern. AI, in his view, will initially benefit whoever controls its major “joints,” while countries with shrinking populations (he points to nations offering incentives for having more children) may deploy human-replacing robotics at scale because they have fewer young workers to protect.

For India — with a large, young population — he argues mindful, sector-by-sector policy choices are essential: deciding deliberately where AI should be deployed (for instance, expert-scarce fields like healthcare) and where it should not be allowed to displace human employment at scale. Blocking new technology outright, he says, risks falling behind globally — but adopting it without a demographic-aware policy risks large-scale domestic unemployment and unrest. AI is a tool. AI is not human. Don't turn a human into a robot.

On the philosophical thread he raised earlier, he adds that whatever comes after AI — “AI, SI, or ASI” — remains a tool; technology can affect ideology and philosophy, but in his view it cannot replace the human search for meaning, which he argues is where Vedantic thought has something distinct to offer.

The stigma around vocational skills — and why it's costing India

Dr. Singh points to a specific cultural block: India's social structure has long stigmatised many skilled trades. He cites tattoo artistry as an example — a trade built into a crore-scale organised business in cities like Mumbai — that in many villages is still dismissed as “that's what the Nat community does,” discouraging families from letting their own children pursue it, even when the child shows real talent and market opportunity exists.

He traces the deeper preference for white-collar, particularly government, jobs — including jobs perceived to carry informal income opportunities — partly to colonial-era bureaucratic conditioning that, in his account, took root under the East India Company and has persisted through independent India's bureaucracy.

The 95% scorer vs the 60% scorer

Sarvesh Mishra raises a question many Indian parents will recognise: society treats a 95% scorer as inherently more intelligent than a 60% scorer, even when the lower scorer is exploring music, sport or other pursuits. Dr. Singh agrees the measurement, not the idea of measurement, is the problem. Assessment today is rote-memory-based; he argues it should evolve to also reflect creativity, analytical ability and exploratory thinking — still expressed as data (since, in his framing, comparative evaluation inevitably requires data), but data that captures a fuller picture of a child's traits, not memory alone.

He references Swami Ramdev as an example of someone whose singular mastery of one discipline — yoga — brought global recognition, arguing that a narrow academic-marks lens would have missed that potential entirely. Related: Acharya Shivam Mishra on yoga teaching as a profession.

A message to parents: stop measuring children only by jobs

Dr. Singh closes with a direct appeal to parents: evaluate a child by more than job prospects. Without cultivating craft, skill, entrepreneurship and values suited to the 21st century, he argues, parents risk imposing a mould on their children rather than nurturing who the child actually is — and destroying originality in children who don't fit that mould. Sarvesh Mishra reinforces this in his closing remark, noting that greed and urgency are what push people to skip the steps that would actually protect their children's futures.

Key takeaways
  • Blame the outdated vision of education, not the youth who passed through it.
  • Macaulay's 1835 reforms still sit behind the “education = government job” equation.
  • Reservation cannot absorb 30 crore youth into 10–15 lakh government openings a year, in the guest's math.
  • A degree divorced from skill and vision is a piece of paper.
  • Vocational training from Class 9–12 and entrepreneurship from Class 6 are already being tried at BSB.
  • AI is a tool; India needs sector-by-sector choices, not panic or blanket adoption.
  • Marks measure memory more than capability.

Frequently asked questions

Is India's youth to blame for graduate unemployment?

No, according to Dr. N.P. Singh. He argues responsibility lies with the generations that shaped the education system's underlying “vision” without updating it for a fast-changing world — not with the young people who went through that system in good faith.

Did Lord Macaulay really design India's current education system?

Dr. Singh states that Macaulay's 1835 reforms replaced the Gurukul tradition and embedded the idea that education exists primarily to secure a (government) job — a framing he says India's system still carries today.

Is reservation the cause of unemployment in India?

Dr. Singh argues no. Using rough figures discussed in the episode — around 30 crore youth in the productive age bracket against roughly 10–15 lakh government job openings annually — he contends even full reservation for any single group wouldn't resolve unemployment, since government jobs are too few relative to the total youth population.

What is the Bharatiya Shiksha Board?

The Bharatiya Shiksha Board (BSB) is a national school education board notified by India's Ministry of Education in 2023, headquartered in Haridwar and associated with Swami Ramdev/Patanjali initiatives. Dr. N.P. Singh serves as its Chairman.

Will AI take away traditional jobs in India?

Dr. Singh believes AI will disrupt many traditional roles, but argues India should respond with mindful, sector-specific policy — deploying AI where expert capacity is scarce (such as healthcare) while protecting employment-heavy sectors, rather than adopting AI uncritically.

What is the difference between Sanatan Dharma as philosophy and as religion, per this episode?

Dr. Singh distinguishes theology (belief accepted on faith) from philosophy (reasoned inquiry into existence and one's relationship to society and nature), arguing Vedantic and allied traditions were originally philosophical and were later narrowed into ritual-based religious identity.

What vocational changes is the Bharatiya Shiksha Board introducing?

Per the episode, BSB has introduced free vocational training (roughly 100 periods a year) from Class 9 to 12 in partnership with government Skill Sector Councils, covering areas like hospitality, green energy, IT, AI assistance and web design, alongside entrepreneurship education starting from Class 6.

Does a high score (95%) mean a student is more capable than one scoring 60%?

Not necessarily, per Dr. Singh. He argues current assessment is heavily memory-based and should evolve to also measure creativity, analytical thinking and exploratory ability — traits a marks-only system can miss entirely.

Watch the full conversation

Strip away the political noise around reservation, and the conversation Sarvesh Mishra and N.P. Singh have is really about a single, uncomfortable question: what is Indian education actually for? Dr. Singh's answer — a system that builds knowledge-creators and lifelong learners rooted in India's own philosophical tradition, not job-seekers trained to memorise and forget — is a demanding one to implement. But as AI accelerates the obsolescence of static skills, the cost of leaving that question unanswered only grows.
