How to Turn Your Farm Into a Factory and Get Rich: IAS Hiralal's "Go Mad, Get Rich" Formula

How can villagers come to believe that farming can bring them both respect and prosperity? On The Sarvesh Mishra Show, Sarvesh Mishra sits with IAS Hira Lal Patel, Secretary in the Uttar Pradesh government and former District Magistrate of Banda. His core message: dream first, get “mad” about becoming wealthy, then use knowledge, a team, processing and marketing to turn your farm into a factory.

Key takeaways
  • “Go mad” is the first condition, because someone obsessed with a goal does not get tired.
  • Grow several commercial crops together instead of one, so income arrives roughly every three months.
  • FPOs (Farmer Producer Organisations) are the answer to small landholdings.
  • Processing (drying, powder, dehydration, frozen) increases both shelf life and value.
  • Learn to sell before you produce: visit the mandi and make traders your partners.
  • Carbon credits are an emerging source of additional income for farmers, in his view.
  • Knowledge now lives on your phone. What matters is the desire to learn and a mentor from your own district.

1. “Go mad, get rich”: it starts with mindset

IAS Hiralal believes that doing big things does not require position, money or power. It requires good intent, good policy and “fire in the belly”. He says that when a person dreams of becoming wealthy every day, the mind starts steering them toward the right learning and activity, and the path begins to appear.

The host raised a fair challenge to the “think and grow rich” style of manifestation: thinking alone does not provide funds or a route. Hiralal’s answer was that the dream and the obsession are only the first step; knowledge, a team and a path must follow. To show that path, he has spent roughly the last year and a half travelling across India every weekend to learn from successful entrepreneurs.

2. Technology, AI and the next three years

In his view, more change will come in the next three years than in the last thirty. People used to drive technology; now technology drives people. He recommended Open to Work, a book by LinkedIn’s CEO, and said that in the coming years AI will take over a large share of jobs. What remains is creativity, and one option is to turn your farm into a factory, draw a salary as its MD and give employment to others.

3. Why don’t government schemes reach the people?

Asked whether the fault lies with the government or the public, his answer was: both. Officials and staff fall short, but people also lack awareness and the willingness to attend trainings and programs. His point: an aware public is very hard for anyone to take advantage of.

He also described using his reputation to help genuine cases, simply asking the officer concerned to look at the papers and do what is right, never to bend the rules.

4. What should a rural unemployed youth do? Become an entrepreneur

For a 25–30 year old without a job, his first answer was not a government scheme but entrepreneurial knowledge:

  • Use free content and certificate courses on social media and YouTube.
  • Choose a successful fish farmer, polyhouse grower or vegetable entrepreneur in your own district as a mentor.
  • Do an internship with them and say: “You became wealthy, please help me do the same.”
  • Today, learning on the ground matters more than joining an institute.

According to him, every district already has around 500 people doing this successfully. The task is to find them and learn from them.

5. The simple company formula: investor + marketer + producer

Once you have knowledge and a dream, build a team of three or four:

  1. An investor, someone with capital.
  2. A marketer, someone who knows how to sell.
  3. A producer, someone who knows how to produce.

“A company simply means bringing three or four people together.” He also offers to serve as an honorary advisor to young entrepreneurs’ companies so that his network and reputation can help them.

6. FPOs: the solution for small landholdings

  • Around 100 small farmers join together to form an FPO.
  • The land holding is counted as the FPO’s, not one farmer’s.
  • Government agencies and private companies buy directly from FPOs, which raises their value.
  • He said central and state governments have supported FPOs with subsidies for the last 5–6 years, and that over 10,000 have been formed. Verify current figures.
  • Speaking about his home district Basti, he noted that many FPOs exist but only some work well.

7. Farming subsidies: where and how?

You can work without subsidies, but if the government is offering them, it makes sense to use them. He explained that the horticulture department and the agriculture department provide various subsidies on items like drip irrigation and processing machines, which farmers often do not know about. Everything is online, and the day you want to know, you will find out.

8. The 5-crop model: how to turn a farm into a factory

He shared a model, suggested by the CEO of an agri company, that he plans to try on his own family farm. Instead of a single crop such as paddy, several commercial crops are grown together:

  • Small marigold: income begins in about 3 months
  • Thai lemon
  • Papaya
  • Moringa
  • Honey boxes along the boundary

The farming is organic, with nutrients delivered through drip irrigation. His illustration: where one acre earned about ₹1 lakh a year, this model could reach ₹5 lakh. Even if the company running the “factory” charges ₹1 lakh as its fee, the farmer’s income still rises and he becomes the owner of the factory. These figures are the guest’s illustrations. Actual results depend on land, weather and market conditions.

Agri companies and models mentioned in the episode

  • Gramik: founded by Raj Yadav of Jaunpur; supports farmers end to end with inputs, advice and buyback. According to the guest, it operates in multiple states and uses AI and a call team to remind farmers about irrigation and medicines on time.
  • FarmersFace: led by Krishna Murari Singh of Siwan; contract farming on about 40 acres of an agricultural university’s land in Kanpur Dehat.
  • Vertex: led by Satyam Bose, who is based in Dubai; running similar experiments in blocks of Prayagraj and Pratapgarh.

These are guest mentions, not endorsements.

9. Processing: from raw produce to real income

Raw vegetables or papaya last only a day or two. After visiting several factories in Pune, he described three processing routes:

  • Frozen food
  • Dried / dehydrated: removing water to extend shelf life up to a year
  • Powder form: for example potato powder, used in food, medicine and cosmetics, and cheaper to transport

He also mentioned a solar dryer machine that works like the old practice of drying vegetables and fruit on the rooftop, but faster and with better quality. According to him, processing also attracts subsidies from the horticulture and agriculture departments.

He spoke about visiting the Spice Board in Kochi and about opportunities to set up processing units in the Spice Park in Lucknow. The host added that renting out machinery could be another rental-income model.

10. Marketing: become a trader first, then a producer

This was the strongest message of the episode. IAS Hiralal says farmers and self-help-group women put all their energy into producing, while the real difficulty is selling.

  • Go to the mandi first and understand wholesalers and retailers.
  • Make commission agents (arhatiyas) your friends and partners.
  • Produce only what you can sell. “If you can sell tomatoes, why are you growing potatoes?”

He told the story of the Sardar and the sacks of potatoes: even by selling at cost, the leftover sacks, the customer relationships and the market knowledge create profit. The host agreed that many businesses make the product first and think about selling later, when the right order is the reverse.

11. The 4-step framework for a farming business

StepWhat to do
1. Mindset changeNot one crop but five; not staying poor but becoming wealthy.
2. ProductionLearn organic, multi-crop farming; find a company or local successful grower as your mentor.
3. ProcessingAdd value through drying, powder, dehydration and frozen products.
4. MarketingVisit the mandi, learn trade, and grow what sells.

12. The role of women

He believes the home itself is a factory, and women have historically run it. In his model, roughly 60% of the work in operations, payments and management of a processing factory would be done by women. The host also pointed to self-help groups and products such as pickles, spices, millets and handicrafts.

13. One Village One Entrepreneur

His campaign idea is to develop at least one willing young entrepreneur in every village. He sought advice from innovation leaders in Chennai and wrote to universities and institutions that train young entrepreneurs. The goal is to create economic hubs inside villages so that people do not have to migrate to cities.

14. Carbon credits

He described carbon credits as agriculture’s equivalent of “AI”. According to him, farmers could get three benefits:

  1. Contributing to slowing climate change.
  2. Additional income from selling carbon credits.
  3. Reviving soil fertility that chemicals have depleted.

He said he has written to agricultural universities urging them to start courses on the subject. Carbon-credit income is not guaranteed.

15. Skills young people need

  • Networking: greet everyone, not only people you need, and maintain relationships.
  • Team building: nobody becomes wealthy without a team.
  • Grounded behaviour: treat people like family, not like a boss.
  • A hunger to learn: the world’s knowledge is on your phone; you only need to learn to use it.

16. Creativity in the age of AI

Towards the end, he said creativity is what AI cannot do. He called for a system or department to nurture creativity, arguing that unemployment, climate change and mental stress can all be tackled through it.

Closing message

Dream, go mad about it, look at successful examples of fish farming, polyhouses, dryers and frozen processing in your own district, learn from them, and grow wealthy while developing your society and country. The host added that “wealth” does not mean greed but prosperity, where a person can fulfil their duties and responsibilities.

Frequently asked questions

Why do government schemes fail to reach ordinary people?

According to IAS Hiralal, both sides share the blame: officials and staff have shortcomings, and citizens often lack awareness and the interest to learn. An aware public is hard for anyone to ignore.

What should an unemployed rural youth do first?

Build entrepreneurial knowledge using free social media content, YouTube and certificate courses. Then pick a successful agri-entrepreneur in your own district as a mentor and do an internship with them.

What can someone with little land or capital do?

Join or form an FPO. Around 100 small farmers come together, and their combined holding is treated as the FPO’s holding. Governments support FPOs with subsidies and assistance, in his account.

Where can farmers get subsidies?

The horticulture and agriculture departments offer subsidies on items such as drip irrigation and processing machinery. The information is available online; what is needed is the desire to find it.

What are the four steps of a farming business?

Mindset change, production (multiple crops), processing, and marketing — learn to sell first, then produce.

What is One Village One Entrepreneur?

An idea to develop at least one willing young entrepreneur in every village, so that each village becomes its own economic hub.

How can carbon credits benefit farmers?

Three benefits were discussed: helping slow climate change, additional income from selling carbon credits, and reviving soil fertility depleted by chemical use. Treat this as a guest view, not a guarantee.
